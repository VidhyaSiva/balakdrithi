What is ABA therapy? A simple guide for parents

If your child has been diagnosed with autism, someone has probably mentioned ABA. Here is what it actually means, in plain words.

Balak Drithi Autism Care · 6-minute read

ABA in one sentence

Applied Behavior Analysis (ABA) is a way of teaching that breaks useful skills into small, clear steps, practises them in a way the child enjoys, and measures progress carefully so the plan keeps improving.

"Behavior" here simply means anything a child does: looking at you, pointing, saying a word, washing hands, waiting for a turn. ABA helps children learn the skills that make everyday life easier and happier.

How does it work?

ABA is built on a simple pattern that therapists call A-B-C:

  • Before (Antecedent): what happens just before, for example the therapist holds up two blocks and asks "Which one?"
  • Behavior: what the child does, for example points to the red block.
  • After (Consequence): what follows, for example "Yes, red!", a smile and a turn with the block. When something good follows, the child is more likely to do it again.

Over hundreds of small, playful moments like this, children learn to ask for things, follow simple instructions, play with others and look after themselves.

Small steps, real progress

A big skill like "ask for what you want" is broken into steps the child can manage: looking, reaching, pointing, then saying the word. Each step is practised until it feels easy, then the next one begins.

Every session is recorded. Therapists note what the child tried, how much help they needed and what worked. A senior clinician (a Board Certified Behavior Analyst, or BCBA) reviews the data regularly and adjusts the plan, so no one is guessing.

What can ABA help with?

Every child is different, so every plan is different. Common goals include:

  • Communication: asking for things, using words, pictures or signs, answering questions.
  • Social and play skills: taking turns, sharing attention, playing alongside and with other children.
  • Daily living: eating, dressing, toileting, washing hands.
  • School readiness: sitting for an activity, following a routine, moving between tasks.
  • Reducing behavior that harms or holds a child back, by first understanding why it happens and then teaching a better way to meet the same need.

Does ABA work?

ABA is one of the most widely studied approaches for young children with autism. The American Academy of Pediatrics notes that interventions work best "when they are early, when they are intense, and when they involve the family", and that families do not need to wait for a formal diagnosis to start therapies such as speech or behavioral support.

ABA is not a cure, and it should never try to make a child "less autistic". Good ABA builds skills that give a child more choice, more independence and more ways to be understood.

An honest note. Some older forms of ABA were rigid and focused on compliance: doing what the adult says, no matter what. Many families and autistic adults have spoken out about this, and the field has listened. Modern, compassionate ABA puts the child's comfort and willingness first. That approach is called assent-based ABA, and it is how we work at Balak Drithi.

What to look for in a good ABA program

  • Supervision by a qualified clinician (a BCBA or equivalent) who reviews your child's progress often.
  • Trained therapists, with real training and shadowing before they work with a child on their own.
  • A plan written for your child, with goals you understand and agree with.
  • Play and joy. Your child should look forward to sessions.
  • Respect for "no". If your child is upset, the therapist pauses and adjusts instead of pushing through.
  • You as a partner, with regular updates and simple ways to practise at home.
