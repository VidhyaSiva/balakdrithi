Why early intervention matters so much

If you are worried about your child's speech, play or behavior, you may hear "Don't worry, they'll grow out of it." Sometimes that's true. But when it isn't, the time lost matters.

Balak Drithi Autism Care · 5-minute read

What is early intervention?

Early intervention means getting the right support for a young child as soon as there are signs of a delay or of autism, usually between ages 2 and 6. It can include behavior therapy (such as ABA), speech therapy, occupational therapy and coaching for parents.

Why earlier is better

The early years are when children learn faster than at any other time in life. Skills build on each other: a child who learns to point can then learn to ask, and a child who can ask can then learn to play and make friends. The earlier a child gets help, the more time those skills have to grow.

Health experts agree:

  • The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) says that when concerns are found early and children get support, "children have better outcomes", and advises against a "wait and see" approach.
  • The American Academy of Pediatrics says interventions work best "when they are early, when they are intense, and when they involve the family".
  • A major report from the US National Research Council recommended that children receive intensive support as soon as autism is recognised.

"Should we wait and see?"

It is natural to hope a child will catch up on their own. But if your child does have autism or a developmental delay, every month of waiting is a month without help. If your child turns out to be fine, getting checked costs you very little.

You don't need a diagnosis to start. The American Academy of Pediatrics notes there is no reason to wait for an autism diagnosis before starting services like speech or behavioral therapy. If something feels off, it is worth acting on.

Signs it's worth talking to someone

  • Few or no words by age 2, or losing words they used to say
  • Not responding to their name, or little eye contact
  • Not pointing to show you things, or not bringing things to share
  • Repeated play, such as lining up toys or spinning objects
  • Very strong reactions to change, sounds, textures or food

These signs don't always mean autism. But they do mean your child deserves a proper look.

What you can do this week

  1. Talk to your pediatrician about your concerns, and ask about a developmental assessment.
  2. Write down what you notice: words, play, sleep, eating, and what upsets your child. Short videos on your phone help professionals too.
